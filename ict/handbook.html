Community norms

  • Ask early. If something is unclear, ask in the live session or by email. There are no silly questions.
  • Respect everyone. Disagree with ideas, never with people. This applies in sessions, peer reviews and on GitHub.
  • Give useful peer feedback. Say what works, what to improve, and why.
  • Credit your sources. Name the people, resources and AI tools you used, and say how you used them.
  • Protect students' data. Never share a child's name, photo or marks in course work.
  • Keep the deadlines in view. The capstone is built in weekly steps; a step missed is hard to recover in a four-week course.

Readings

Textbook: Bates, A. W. (2022). Teaching in a Digital Age (3rd ed.). Tony Bates Associates. Free and openly licensed: read it online. The section numbers below follow the digital copy shared with you.

Each week pairs textbook sections with a few references for what the textbook does not cover.

Week 1

Textbook, Sections 7.1 and 8.1: Choosing technologies for teaching and learning: the challenge (pp. 437–441); Thinking about the pedagogical differences of media (pp. 501–510) Reading time: about 25 minutes

Reading guide

Key points

  • Choosing is harder than it looks. “Technology” is not just a device: it is the hardware, the software, the network and the service behind it. People learn well without any technology too. Teachers get stuck between using every new tool because it is new and rejecting them all because it feels too complicated. So we need a way to decide. (7.1)
  • Start with the teaching, not the tool. Bates gives five questions: how you believe students learn, the outcomes you want, your teaching methods, what each medium does well, and what resources you have. Ask them in a cycle: a medium you find may change the outcomes you first planned. (8.1.2)
  • Match the medium to three things: the content, how that content is structured, and the skills students need. Books, radio and face-to-face teaching present content one step after another. Computers can show many things changing at once. (8.1.3)
  • Affordances: a word from the psychologist James Gibson. A door knob suggests turning and a flat plate suggests pushing. A medium’s affordances are the teaching possibilities it opens up. Bates prefers to ask what a medium does that others find hard, such as slow motion in video. (8.1.4)

Read closely: 7.1, pp. 439–440 (what counts as technology; why we need decision models). 8.1.2 to 8.1.4, pp. 502–506 (the five questions, the three things to match, affordances).

Skim: 8.1.1 (p. 501, a link back to Chapter 7) and 8.1.5 (pp. 506–507, why Bates gives no fixed list of affordances).

Skip: 8.1.6 (a short podcast, only in the online version) and the reference list (pp. 507–510).

Do: Activity 7.1 on p. 441. Answer question 1 for yourself: how do you decide today which technology to use? Keep your answer. You will come back to it in the case studies.

Watch with it: the ViBe lesson “Choosing technologies (Bates 7.1 and 8.1)”.

  • Koehler, M. J., & Mishra, P. (2009). What is technological pedagogical content knowledge? (TPACK) Reading time: about 30 minutes
    Reading guide

    Key points

    • Digital tools make teaching harder. A pencil or a microscope does one job, hardly changes, and you can see how it works. Digital tools can be used in many ways, change all the time, and hide how they work. (p. 61)
    • Tools are not neutral. Each one makes some things easy (its affordances) and some things hard (its constraints). Email is good for messages people read later, but poor at carrying tone and feeling. (p. 61)
    • Three kinds of teacher knowledge: content (what you teach), pedagogy (how you teach) and technology (what you teach with), and the overlaps between them. (pp. 62–65)
    • The same tool can be used very differently. A whiteboard at the front of a classroom belongs to the teacher; in an office brainstorm, anyone picks up the pen. Most software is built for offices, not classrooms, so teachers have to look past a tool’s usual use. (pp. 65–66)
    • TPACK is what you know when all three work together in your own classroom. There is “no single technological solution” for every teacher or course, and a change in any one of the three has to be balanced by changes in the other two. (pp. 66–67)

    Read closely: pp. 61–62 (why teaching with technology is hard) and pp. 65–67 (TCK, TPK and TPACK).

    Skim: pp. 63–64 (content, pedagogical and technology knowledge on their own; the TPACK lesson on ViBe covers these) and the Implications section on p. 67.

    Skip: the reference list (pp. 68–70).

    Do: think of one tool your own teachers used, even a blackboard or a textbook diagram. Write one thing it made easy and one thing it made hard.

    Watch with it: the ViBe lessons “The TPACK framework” and “TPACK: a reflection”. You will use this reading in a case study.

  • OECD. (2015). Students, Computers and Learning: Making the Connection. Read the executive summary. Reading time: about 15 minutes
    Reading guide

    Key points

    • The question: does using computers at school help students learn? The report uses PISA 2012 data on 15-year-olds. In OECD countries, 96% had a computer at home but only 72% used one at school, and two of the top performers, Korea and Shanghai-China, used them at school the least. (p. 15)
    • No easy payoff. Countries that invested heavily in computers for schools saw “no appreciable improvements” in reading, maths or science. Some computer use at school went with better results than none, but heavy use went with poorer results. (pp. 15–16)
    • Basic skills first. Reading online needs the same skills as reading print, plus finding your way and judging sources, and these can be taught with ordinary tools too. Making sure every child can read and do maths does more for fairness than handing out devices. (pp. 15–16)
    • Watch the risks. Students online for more than six hours on a weekday outside school were more likely to feel lonely at school and to skip school. (p. 16)
    • The lesson for teachers: it takes time and effort to learn to use technology well while staying focused on learning. “Technology can amplify great teaching, but great technology cannot replace poor teaching.” (p. 17)

    Read closely: pp. 15–17. This is the whole executive summary, about three pages.

    Skim: Tables 0.1 to 0.6 (pp. 18–26) if you like numbers. India did not take part in PISA 2012, so it is not in the tables.

    Skip: the rest of the report.

    Do: think back to your own school. Was technology used too little, about right, or too much for learning? Note one example.

    Watch with it: a ViBe lesson on this reading is coming. You will use this reading in a case study.

  • Ministry of Education. (2020). National Education Policy 2020, Chapter 23 (Technology use and integration). Reading time: about 15 minutes
    Reading guide

    Key points

    • Technology and education help each other. Technology can improve teaching, and education builds a digitally able India. (23.1)
    • Test before scaling up. New technology will be used only if it is “rigorously and transparently evaluated” in the right setting first. A national forum, the NETF, will give governments independent, evidence-based advice. The NETF is a plan in the policy; it has no official website yet. (23.3)
    • What technology is for: better teaching, learning and evaluation; teacher training; reaching more students; and easier school management. (23.5)
    • Content for everyone: in all major Indian languages, usable by students in remote areas and Divyang students, shared on DIKSHA, with equipment so teachers can use it in class. (23.6)
    • Teach thinking, not just facts. Computers already handle facts and procedures well, yet schools overload students with them instead of building higher-order skills. (23.7)

    Read closely: 23.1 to 23.3 and 23.5 to 23.7 (pp. 56–57).

    Skim: 23.4 (how the NETF works), 23.8 and 23.9 (AI and research), 23.12 and 23.13 (teaching about new technologies, AI ethics and data privacy; these come back in Week 3).

    Skip: 23.10 and 23.11 (university courses and research degrees).

    Do: pick one technology your school or college brought in. Was it tested before everyone had to use it? How would you know whether it worked?

    Watch with it: a ViBe lesson on this reading is coming.

Week 2

Textbook, Chapter 10: Choosing and using media in education (the SECTIONS model). Skim it for the main idea; you do not need to remember the details. Reading time: about 20 minutes (skim)

Reading guide

Key points

  • There is no formula. Choosing media involves many things that affect each other, and no accepted theory tells you the answer. SECTIONS is a set of questions to ask, not a calculator: Students, Ease of use, Costs, Teaching functions, Interaction, Organisational issues, Networking, Security and privacy. (pp. 613–614, 618)
  • Start with the students, and above all access. Bates calls access “perhaps the most discriminating” question: however good a medium is, students cannot learn from it if they cannot get to it easily and cheaply. Know who your students are, what devices they have and what skills they bring. (10.2.2, 10.2.4)
  • Keep it simple and reliable. Technology is usually a means, not the subject. Time spent learning a tool or fixing it is time taken from learning. Wait for new tools to settle before using them in class: better “the leading edge” than “the bleeding edge”. (10.3)
  • Cost is mostly your time. Making and sharing media has become much cheaper, so cost alone rarely decides. Count your time over the whole course, not just the hours to prepare. (10.4.1, 10.4.5)
  • Teaching value decides less than you think. Good teachers make up for a weak medium, and hard-working students make up for poor teaching with it. So access and ease of use often matter more than how good a medium is for teaching. Still, use what each medium does best. (10.5)
  • Students learn best when they are active. How you design the activity matters more than which medium you pick. (10.6.5)
  • The school shapes the choice. Teachers use what their school supports and trains them for, and schools favour tools that need the least change. “N” now stands for networking: should students connect with people beyond the class? (10.7.1, 10.8.1)
  • Then decide with your judgement. Bates does not add up scores. Ask the questions, then trust a well-informed gut decision: “rough but ready usually beats perfect but late.” The choice of media sits inside the design of the whole lesson or course. (10.10)

Focus on: what the eight SECTIONS questions ask (pp. 613–614 and 618) and how Bates uses them to decide (10.10.1 and 10.10.2, pp. 689–694). Aim to come away able to use the questions on a lesson of your own.

Skim: the opening paragraph of each of 10.2 to 10.8, one per letter of SECTIONS, and the summary of 10.6 (p. 668).

Skip: everything else, including 10.1.1 (older models), the costing of university online courses (10.4.2 to 10.4.4), 10.9 (you read it in Week 3), 10.10.3, 10.11 (SAMR) and all reference lists.

Do: before you read, Activity 10.1 (p. 621): pick a lesson you may teach in school and note which media you would use and why. After you read, Activity 10.10 (p. 695): answer the SECTIONS questions for the same lesson. What changed in your list, and why?

Watch with it: the ViBe lesson on the SECTIONS model (Bates Chapter 10).

Textbook, Section 3.5.3.1: Laboratory, workshop or studio work (pp. 180–182) Reading time: about 15 minutes

Reading guide

Key points

  • What labs are for. Bates lists seven goals: using real equipment well, building hand skills with tools, understanding “the advantages and limitations of laboratory experiments”, seeing science at work, testing whether an idea holds up, learning to design and run experiments, and making objects. (pp. 180–181)
  • Why labs matter. They help students move from the concrete (what they see happen) to the abstract (the principle behind it). They also teach a habit at the heart of science: an idea counts as ‘true’ only after it has been tested carefully. (p. 181)
  • The problem with labs. School labs can rarely offer the equipment scientists use today, and labs cost a lot in money and staff time, so they are hard to provide for every student. (p. 181)
  • Start from the purpose. Some science can only be observed remotely or digitally. Be clear about what a lab is meant to achieve, because there may now be “more practical, more economic, or more powerful ways” of achieving it, such as remote labs and simulations. (pp. 181–182)

Read closely: all of 3.5.3.1 (pp. 180–182). It is short, and Case 3 uses it.

Skim: 3.5.1 and 3.5.2 (pp. 178–179) for the bigger idea it belongs to: learning by doing, and Kolb’s four stages of learning from experience.

Skip: the history of teaching labs (Huxley and the 1860s, p. 181).

Do: think of one experiment from Class 9 or 10 science. Go through Bates’s seven goals and mark which a simulation could meet and which need real equipment. Keep your list for Case 3.

Try it: PhET’s free Circuit Construction Kit, which Case 3 is about.

Warschauer, M. (2003). Technology and Social Inclusion: Rethinking the Digital Divide. MIT Press, free to read online. Introduction (pp. 1–6) and Chapter 2 (pp. 47–48) Reading time: about 20 minutes

Reading guide

Key points

  • Three projects that gave people computers. A computer kiosk in a New Delhi slum, the “Hole-in-the-Wall” (2000). An Irish town, Ennis, that won a prize and planned a computer for every family (1997). A computer lab donated to an Egyptian college of education. Each had money and good intentions, and each ran into trouble. (pp. 1–5)
  • What went wrong at the wall. The internet seldom worked, nothing was in Hindi, the only language the children knew, and there were no teachers. The children mostly drew and played games. Parents came to see “minimally invasive education” as “minimally effective education”. (pp. 1–2)
  • Less money, better results. Three runner-up towns in Ireland got one-fifteenth of Ennis’s prize. They planned, trained people and worked with local groups, and had more to show for it than Ennis. (pp. 3–4)
  • The common problem. These projects focused on hardware and software and paid too little attention to the human and social systems that must also change. Meaningful access is “far more than merely providing computers and Internet connections”. (p. 6)
  • Not haves and have-nots. Access comes in degrees, not as a yes-or-no split. (p. 6)
  • Four resources. Physical (devices and connections), digital (content), human (literacy and education) and social (community and institutions that support use). (pp. 47–48)
  • They feed each other. Each resource helps people use technology well, and using it well builds more of each. Handled well, this is a virtuous circle; handled poorly, a vicious cycle of exclusion. (p. 48)

Read closely: pp. 1–2 (the Hole-in-the-Wall), p. 6 (rethinking the digital divide) and pp. 47–48 (the four resources).

Skim: pp. 2–5 (Ireland and Egypt).

Skip: pp. 7–9 and the rest of the book.

Do: think of one technology your school or college brought in. For each of the four resources, was it there? Keep your notes for Case 4.

Watch with it: the ViBe lesson “Access is not use”.

Textbook, Section 8.4: Video (pp. 527–536)

In short (one paragraph)

What can video do that text and audio cannot? It shows things moving, links concrete examples to abstract principles, and brings in events too dangerous, costly or far away to visit. Bates lists its strengths (plenty of free material, students can pause and do an activity, phones make it cheap to film) and its weaknesses (finding the right clip takes time, copyright, students need activities around the video, and many prefer being told to working things out). His conclusion: video is underused, and too often it just records a lecture. It works when it is planned into the lesson, with preparation before and follow-up after. Read it if you plan to use or make videos for your classes.

Textbook, Section 8.5: Computing (pp. 537–544)

In short (one paragraph)

By “computing” Bates means using a computer to design or carry out learning activities, not writing code. He lists what it does well (combines text, audio and video, gives instant feedback, lets students learn anywhere, takes over routine marking) and where it falls short (teachers lack training, some students lack devices or skills, it is oversold as a cure for everything, and some learning still needs teacher and students together, more so for younger learners). His main point is about control: computing works best when teachers decide when and how to use it. Read it if you have wondered whether computers will replace teachers.

Textbook, Chapter 11: Modes of delivery (pp. 701–752)

In short (one paragraph)

Courses sit somewhere on a line from fully face-to-face, through blended, to fully online. Bates admits there is no good theory for deciding where a course should sit, so he offers four things to weigh: your students’ needs, the way you prefer to teach, what the subject needs (its content and skills), and your resources, including your own time. He also asks what a campus is for once most content can be found online. The chapter is long and written for colleges and universities, but the four factors apply when a school plans blended or online lessons. Read it if you want a way to decide what to teach in class and what to put online. Start with the Key Takeaways on p. 702.

Textbook, Section 12.2: Open educational resources (pp. 767–776)

In short (one paragraph)

Open educational resources (OER) are teaching materials that are free to use: textbooks, videos, simulations, slides, even tests. Bates explains the 5Rs of openness (reuse, redistribute, revise, remix, retain), how Creative Commons licences tell you what you may do with a resource, where to find OER, and their main limit, which is uneven quality. His view: it makes no sense to make everything from scratch when good free material exists. This textbook is itself an OER. Read it if you want to use or share materials legally, for example in your capstone.

Ministry of Education. (2020). National Education Policy 2020, Chapter 24: Online and digital education (pp. 58–60). Same link as Week 1.

In short (one paragraph)

Written after COVID-19 closed schools, this chapter asks how to use online and digital education without leaving anyone behind. It says the benefits cannot be gained unless the digital divide is closed; that a good classroom teacher is not automatically a good online teacher; and that online learning not blended with activity-based learning becomes “screen-based education”. It then lists ten initiatives, including pilot studies, DIKSHA and SWAYAM, TV and radio for homes without internet, virtual labs, and blended models of learning. Read it if you want the policy behind this week’s ideas on access and labs. It takes about 10 minutes.

Ministry of Human Resource Development. (2020). PRAGYATA: Guidelines for Digital Education (39 pp.)

In short (one paragraph)

National guidelines, prepared by NCERT in 2020 while schools were closed. They say schools should not try to recreate the classroom online, and they name the eight PRAGYATA steps: Plan, Review, Arrange, Guide, Yak (talk), Assign, Track, Appreciate. A Class 4 example (pp. 8–9) shows a teacher who surveys families’ devices, from homes with a laptop to one with no phone at all, and plans differently for each. The guidelines also set screen-time limits: on online-class days, Classes 1 to 8 should have no more than two live sessions of 30–45 minutes (p. 19). Later sections cover cyber safety, children with special needs, and health. Read it if you may teach where families have very different access to devices.

Week 3

Cambridge Assessment International Education. Getting started with Assessment for Learning. Three parts: “What is assessment for learning?”, “Six misconceptions about AFL”, and comment-only marking (in “Assessment for learning in practice”, part 2, and “Next steps”, idea 2) Reading time: about 15 minutes

Reading guide

Key points

  • What it is. Assessment for learning (AFL) creates feedback that is then used to improve students’ work. It aims to ‘close the gap’ between where a learner is now and where they want to be. (What is AFL?)
  • Purpose, not form. “It is the purpose of assessment, rather than the nature of it, that is important.” Even a test or exam supports AFL if its results are used to help students improve. (What is AFL?)
  • Five processes. Questioning to find out where a student is; feedback on how to improve; knowing what good work looks like; peer and self-assessment; and using summative tests formatively. (What is AFL?)
  • Assessment is not the same as testing. Any activity that checks how well a student is learning is assessment, and its information is used to adapt the teaching. (Misconceptions)
  • Grades can crowd out comments. Learners “will often just read the grade and ignore the comments”. If you give a grade, let them read the comments and improve the work first. (Misconceptions)
  • Comment-only marking. Give a specific comment instead of a grade, tied to success criteria the learners know. Give the work back, let them improve it, and only then share a grade. Feedback should be about the task (“now make the main character more striking”), not about the person (“the best in the class”). (In practice, 2; Next steps, 2)

Read closely: “What is assessment for learning?” (up to the first video), the six misconceptions, and the two comment-only marking parts.

Skim: the rest of “Assessment for learning in practice” (questioning, peer and self-assessment) and the AFL checklist.

Skip: the theory section (Hattie, attribution theory), the challenges, the videos and the glossary.

Do: think of the last test you took at school or college. What did you do with it when it came back: read the comments, or look at the mark and put it away? Keep your answer for Case 5.

Mollick, E., & Mollick, L. (2023). Using AI to implement effective teaching strategies in classrooms: Five strategies, including prompts. SSRN (free to download). pp. 1–2 and 7–8 Reading time: about 15 minutes

Reading guide

Key points

  • Good strategies, too little time. Many teaching strategies work well but take too long for a busy teacher to prepare. AI can make material for five of them: many examples, many explanations, low-stakes tests, checks on what students know, and spaced practice. (pp. 1–2)
  • A word of caution. The teacher’s subject and teaching knowledge is still needed to judge what the AI writes. It “can hallucinate (make up facts)”. Used with care, AI can be a “force multiplier” for a teacher. (p. 2)
  • Questions to check AI examples. Are they relevant? Factually correct? Detailed enough? Interesting? Varied? Do they connect the idea to real life? Examples can be “subtly wrong and thereby teach the wrong lesson”. (p. 7)
  • Turn wrong examples into a task. Students who know the topic can be asked which examples show the idea and which do not, and why. (p. 7)
  • Many explanations. Writing several explanations of one idea, at different levels, is hard and slow. AI can produce them, or a simpler summary for a confused student, but every one “must be vetted by the instructor before they reach students.” (pp. 7–8)
  • Steps for a prompt. Name the concept, say what you need, describe the style and the audience (for example, Class 8 students new to the idea), then ask for analogies. (p. 8)

Read closely: p. 2 (from “A word of caution”) and pp. 7–8.

Skim: p. 1 (the abstract) and the start of p. 2.

Skip: the rest of the paper. It is optional (see Going further). The prompts name tools from 2023 (Bing, ChatGPT); the ideas apply to any AI tool.

Do: ask any AI tool for three examples of a concept you may teach in school. Check them with the questions on p. 7. Is any of them wrong, or subtly wrong? Keep your notes for Case 6.

CAST. (2024). Universal Design for Learning Guidelines, version 3.0. Guideline 1, “Design options for perception”, with Considerations 1.1 and 1.2 Reading time: about 15 minutes

Reading guide

Key points

  • Why perception comes first. “Learning is impossible if information is imperceptible to the learner”, and difficult when it needs extraordinary effort or help to perceive. (Guideline 1)
  • Three ways to reduce the barrier. Offer the same information through different senses (sight, hearing, touch), in formats the learner can adjust (text that can be enlarged, sound that can be made louder), and from a variety of perspectives. (Guideline 1)
  • Good for many, not only a few. Multiple representations help learners with sensory disabilities “but also support access and comprehension for many others.” (Guideline 1)
  • Let learners adjust the display. Print is fixed; digital material can let learners change text size, spacing, colour, contrast, volume and speed. But digital does not mean accessible: many digital materials leave these features out. (1.1)
  • Do not rely on one sense. Describe images, graphs and videos in words; give captions or transcripts for speech and video; offer text-to-speech. Directions given only aloud are a barrier for some learners. (1.2)

Read closely: the Guideline 1 page and Considerations 1.1 and 1.2.

Skim: Consideration 1.3 (diverse perspectives and identities).

Skip: the other eight guidelines and the research pages, for now.

Do: take one page of notes or slides you have made. Who in a class of 40 could not see, hear or read it easily? Name one change from 1.1 or 1.2 that would help. Keep your notes for Case 8 and the Week 4 accessibility check.

Government of India. (2023). Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. Section 9 (p. 8) and three meanings in Section 2: “child”, “personal data” and “processing” (pp. 2–3) Reading time: about 10 minutes

Reading guide

Key points

  • Who is a child. Anyone who has not completed eighteen years. So every school student is a child under this law. (s. 2(f))
  • What counts as personal data. Any data about a person who can be identified from it, such as a name, a phone number or a photo in which a child can be recognised. (s. 2(t))
  • What counts as processing. Almost anything done with digital data: collecting, storing, using, sharing, and “dissemination or otherwise making available”. Posting online is processing. (s. 2(x))
  • Parent’s consent first. Before processing a child’s personal data, an organisation must get “verifiable consent of the parent” (or lawful guardian). (s. 9(1))
  • No harm, no tracking. No processing that is likely to harm a child’s well-being, and no tracking, behavioural monitoring or targeted advertising aimed at children. (s. 9(2), 9(3))
  • When it applies. These duties start on 14 May 2027, under the DPDP Rules, 2025. The Rules also let schools track and monitor children for their educational activities and for children’s safety; the no-harm rule still applies. Good practice need not wait for the deadline.

Read closely: Section 9 (p. 8), and the three meanings in Section 2 (f), (t) and (x) (pp. 2–3).

Skim: Section 2 (i) and (j): who a “Data Fiduciary” (the organisation) and a “Data Principal” (the person, or a child’s parent) are.

Skip: the rest of the Act.

Do: list three ways a school you know uses children’s data on phones or computers (for example, a WhatsApp group, an attendance app, photos on a page). For each, who would need to give consent? Keep your list for Case 7 and the Week 4 privacy check.

Textbook, Section 6.8: Assessment of learning (pp. 413–422)

In short (one paragraph)

Bates starts from the point that nothing drives what students do more than how they are assessed. He lists the purposes of assessment (improving learning, checking outcomes, feedback to the teacher, selecting people for further study or jobs) and looks at how technology changes the methods: computer-marked multiple-choice tests (cheap and good for facts, weak for higher-level skills), written answers, peer assessment, project work, e-portfolios, simulations and games, and online proctoring. His view is that in a digital age, teaching and assessment become closely joined. Read it if you want more on choosing an assessment method than Case 5 gives you.

Textbook, Section 10.9: Security and privacy (pp. 682–688)

In short (one paragraph)

This is the “S” in SECTIONS. Teachers and students need a private place to work online, and schools want to protect students from data collection by companies, from tracking and from online bullying. Bates explains why password-protected systems helped, why cloud services and social media make this harder (data may sit in another country, under its laws), and why privacy is a matter of policy, not only of technology. His examples are from Canada and the USA. He ends with three questions: what student information must I keep private, what risk does this tool bring, and what must stay behind closed doors? Read it if you want a teacher’s view to go with the DPDP Act for your Week 4 privacy check.

Mollick, E., & Mollick, L. (2023): the rest of the paper (pp. 3–6 and 9–23)

In short (one paragraph)

The remaining strategies, each with ready-made prompts and advice on checking what the AI gives back: many varied examples (pp. 3–6), more on explanations and analogies (p. 9), low-stakes tests (pp. 11–15), quick checks of what students found most important or most confusing, with AI summarising their answers (pp. 16–18), and spaced practice that brings back earlier topics (pp. 19–22). The prompts name 2023 tools, but they work with any AI chatbot. Read it if you want prompts to try when you plan lessons for your capstone. If you paste students’ answers into an AI tool, think about their privacy first.

UNESCO. (2023). Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research (44 pp.)

In short (one paragraph)

UNESCO’s first global guidance on generative AI in education. It explains in plain terms how GenAI works, then lists its risks: a wider digital divide, content used without consent, answers no one can explain, AI-made text filling the internet, no real understanding of the world, fewer different opinions, and deepfakes (pp. 14–17). It asks governments to protect users’ data and says the minimum age for using GenAI on one’s own should be 13 (p. 21). It asks schools to check tools before using them and to protect human agency: people, not AI, should stay in charge of thinking and decisions. Tables in Section 5 suggest uses for teachers and learners. Read it if you want the reasons behind the rules in the ViBe lesson on using GenAI safely.

Case studies

A case is a short story about a teacher who has to choose between two ways of teaching a topic, often with a different tool or with no tool at all. Each case comes with one reading from the course. There is no single right answer. What we look at is how well you reason: whether you use the reading, and whether you are fair to the choice you did not make.

Where: samagama.in/hb302. You sign in with your IIT Ropar Google account. The first case opens in class on Thursday 29 October. All case dates: Case study dates.

What you do for each case

  1. Read the case and its reading.
  2. Talk it over, if you like. You are free to discuss the case with your classmates before you write, in class, in your group or anywhere else. Hearing how someone else sees it often sharpens your own view. Then write your response on your own, in your own words.
  3. Write your response (150 to 400 words). Choose one of the two options and follow the "How to respond" steps at the end of the case. Write it yourself: no AI. Type with the keyboard: copying, pasting and voice typing are turned off. If you leave the page, your writing session ends, but your draft is saved.
  4. Review three classmates' responses after the response deadline. You will not see their names, and they will not see yours. For each one you say which option the writer chose, say in one sentence what their choice loses, rate how well it uses the reading, rate how fairly it makes the case for the other option, and write a comment of at least 40 words: one thing it does well and one thing that would make it stronger.
  5. Read the comments on your own response. You see your classmates' comments only, not their ratings or names.

How your case marks are worked out

  • Case studies are 20% of the course. There are 8 cases and your best 6 count, so each counted case is worth one sixth of the 20 marks. Doing Cases 1 to 6 completes the requirement; Cases 7 and 8 are optional.
  • 70% of each case's marks come from the ratings your three reviewers give your response: how well it uses the reading, and how fairly it makes the case for the other option. The middle of the three ratings is used, so one very harsh or very generous reviewer does not decide your mark.
  • 30% of each case's marks are for your own reviewing: each of your three reviews counts if you submit it before the review deadline and correctly name the option the writer chose. Read the response carefully before you rate it.
  • The teaching team reads the reviews. If a reviewer gives top ratings to almost everything, we look at those reviews ourselves. A review that does not match what the response says is removed and earns no reviewing marks. Rate what you read, not what is kind to a friend.
  • Late responses and appeals follow the Deadlines rules: a late response is reviewed by the teaching team and gets no reviewing marks, and each approved appeal costs 10% of that case's marks.

To do well: use the case's reading to make your argument, not only to name it, and give the strongest reason for the option you did not choose, as its best supporter would put it.

Course rules

These rules are here so that everyone knows what to expect from the start. If something in your life makes one of them hard to meet, write to us early. It is much easier to help before a deadline than after it.

What you need to complete the course

  • All the ViBe lecture videos, watched in full.
  • The case studies: at least 6 of the 8, each with your response and your three peer reviews.
  • The live sessions: you may miss at most 2 sessions over the whole course. Use them for days you are unwell or something unavoidable comes up.

Deadlines

We do not give extensions. The course is only four weeks long, and in the case studies your classmates are waiting to review your work, so one late response holds up others.

  • Late case response: you can still submit until the review deadline of that case. It is then reviewed by the teaching team instead of by classmates, and you do not get the reviewing marks for that case.
  • Missed peer reviews: you can appeal to reopen them. If the instructor approves, each approved appeal costs 10% of that case's marks. One appeal per case.

Using AI

  • Case studies: no AI. Do not use AI to write your responses or your peer reviews. The point of a case is your own reasoning as a future teacher, and your classmates are reviewing what you think.
  • Capstone: AI is welcome. You will build with AI coding tools ("vibe coding"). Say which tools you used and how, as the community norms ask.

Academic integrity

You are training to be a teacher, and your students will learn honesty partly from how you work. These rules follow the IIT Ropar academic rules; read the institute handbook as well.

  • Your work is your own. Do not copy, buy or reuse someone else's work and submit it as yours. This covers case responses, peer reviews, your Tenali work and your capstone.
  • Case studies are individual. Do not share your case response or peer reviews with classmates, and do not copy theirs. Letting someone copy your work is also malpractice.
  • Group work is shared, not divided up blindly. In your Tenali pair or group, every member should be able to explain the contribution the group made.
  • Review honestly. Mark a classmate's response for what it says, not for who wrote it. Do not agree on marks with each other.
  • Credit sources and AI. Name every source and AI tool you used, and follow the AI rules above: no AI in the case studies.
  • Keep course spaces closed. Do not share Zoom links, logins or classmates' work outside the class.

What happens when these rules are broken (penalties) will be added here.
